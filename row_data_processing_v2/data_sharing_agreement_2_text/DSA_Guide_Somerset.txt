1 Introduction
Welcome to your sharing journey
To make informed decisions about safeguarding and
achieve quality outcomes for children and families,
we have to share information either internally
or with our partner agencies who also have
responsibilities for safeguarding and prevention.
It has been frequently recognised in local and national
reviews of practice that failing to share information
at critical times has costs lives or led to detrimental
outcomes. A fear of sharing sensitive information must not
be a blocker to safeguarding and promoting the welfare of
children and families at risk. The relevant UK government
departments and independent non-departmental
government bodies responsible for protecting children
and/or regulating data protection therefore set out
policy, legislation and statutory guidance on how the
child protection system should work and data protection
compliance is achieved at the same time. Those pieces
of legislation are in place for local authorities and their
sharing partners to be used to justify information sharing
and to allow it in a safe and legal way. Some legislation
puts a duty on us to share and others provide us with
power to do so.
What is safeguarding?
The Care Quality Commission (CQC) describes
safeguarding as protecting people’s health, wellbeing and
human rights, and enabling them to live free from harm,
abuse and neglect. It’s fundamental to high-quality health
and social care. The Department for Education defines
safeguarding as follows within their ‘Working Together
to Safeguard Children’ guide to inter-agency working to
safeguard and promote the welfare of children:
a. Protecting children from maltreatment
b. Preventing impairment of children’s mental and
physical health or development
c. Ensuring that children are growing up in
circumstances consistent with the provision of safe
and effective care
d. Taking action to enable all children to have the
best outcomes
Safeguarding must therefore be seen as a protection of
wellbeing (including physical, mental & emotional); a
prevention of harm and reduction of risk through care
and support requiring information sharing. This allows
intervention in immediate situations demanding the
safeguarding of children and individuals but also sharing
for prevention and early intervention in less immediate or
high-risk situations.

First steps to get started
Our guidance and template have been designed to
take you through this journey. It not only provides
a template but also guidance including examples of
what is needed, technical advice and signposting to
other key documents which can help you.
Here is a simple flow chart of what you will need to do to
go from having concerns about sharing data to sharing
data legally and securely with confidence.
1. Why is the information needed?
What is the purpose for sharing the relevant information, think about the purpose for individuals, your organisation
and the wider public.
2. What information is needed?
Be specific and descriptive, consider how often it is required, refer to the data item template available under 5.
‘Information to be shared by partners’ within the guidance document.
3. What organisation can provide the information?
Have you explored if the information is available already, maybe in other parts of your organisation. Have you spoken to
a counterpart in the potential sharing organisation who can advise you on what information is available and how often.
4. Have you completed a Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA)?
Be aware that a DPIA is likely to be a legal requirement. Complete it before you start processing any data. Use the
guidance document to learn more about the requirement to complete a DPIA.
5. How will it be transferred?
Consider your options and assess the risk of those. Transfers must be safe and secure, consult with your technical teams
for more complex digital solutions (e.g. data transfer system to system or via Secure File Transfer Protocol [SFTP]).
6. Where will it be held?
Consider your options and assess the risk of those. Any information must be held safe and secure, consult with your
technical teams for more complex digital solutions.

7. Are you sure the information is accurate and not misleading?
Take all reasonable steps to ensure the personal data you hold is not incorrect or misleading. If you discover that
personal data is incorrect or misleading you must take steps to correct or erase it as soon as possible. Carefully consider
any challenges to the accuracy of personal data.
8. How will you process it?
Define what solutions are available to process the information (e.g. data warehouse, modelling, risk scoring, manual
usage to inform cases), work closely with the relevant teams (e.g. analytics, IT, ethics).
9. How long will you keep it?
Follow your internal retention policy and establish how long you actually need the information. Include any potential
outcome products and long term requirements to hold the data.
10. How will you delete the data?
Follow your internal records retention or data destruction policy to assure safe destruction of the information you hold.
Consider the method depending on your storage solution to allow for safety of destruction.
The UK GDPR sets out seven key principles which should lie at the heat of your approach to processing personal data:
● Lawfulness, fairness and transparency
● Purpose limitation
● Data minimisation
● Accuracy
● Storage limitation
● Integrity and confidentiality (security)
● Accountability
Please visit the ICO website for more detail on the principles.

What is next
Once you have answered the essential questions,
established details, and maybe started to complete
your Data Protection Impact Assessment you can
start to think about completing the Safeguarding
Data Sharing Agreement template.
We hope this introduction, the Data Sharing Agreement
template and accompanying guidance give confidence
in pursuing the important journey to sharing relevant
safeguarding information and reduces the fear of the
unknown. You might want to start with the glossary,
if you glance through it now, it will help you going
through the template. And don’t forget the help available
inside (Caldicott Guardian, information governance
(IG) colleagues, Data Protection Officer, colleagues with
experience of information sharing) and outside (we have
provided a page with relevant links to valuable information
within the guidance document) your organisation.
This Data Sharing Agreement template should be read
and used alongside the guidance document. Each section
of the DSA is accompanied by the relevant examples of
completed DSAs, example scenarios and guidance for
completing the template. Data Protection Officer advice
and templates for relevant appendices are also included.
This Data Sharing Agreement Template and Guidance has
been developed to:
1. Help agencies and organisations to gain
confidence and understanding of sharing for
safeguarding purposes through the spectrum of
sharing scenarios considering:
● Sharing with individual or multiple sharing partners
● Different types of partners (e.g. police, education,
housing)
● Information sharing of specific data items (e.g. name
and relevant services open to) to mass data sharing for
analytical and risk modelling purposes
● Simple exchanges of data to the use of complex data
warehouses
● Sharing for immediate safeguarding needs or early
intervention and prevention
2. Assist agencies and organisations by providing
complex, specialised information governance
requirements in an easily accessible and
understandable way tackling issues like:
● Lack of expertise and knowledge
● Under resourced IG teams and relevant professional
subject matter experts
● General resource and available time to research data
protection requirements

3. Clarify commonly held misconceptions, such as:
● Not being able (allowed) to share
● Needing consent to share
● Only being able to share with statutory bodies
4. Provide a Safeguarding Data Sharing Agreement
Template and a corresponding Guidance Document
with the required help for all levels of staff
involved with an Information Sharing Initiative,
including front line practitioners receiving and
making data requests, through to Data Protection

The Template and Guidance will lead you through
the relevant sections and considerations required to
allow sharing and to complete the Safeguarding Data
Sharing Agreement. Alternatively if you already have
such agreements, it can be used to quality assure
existing approaches.
It will assist by going through the relevant steps of
consideration:
● What information are you requesting to receive or share
and what is the purpose for doing so?
● What organisations are involved and what are their

● Is a Data Protection Impact Assessment required and has
it been completed (since the sharing is about children it

● Is the sharing justified (agree this in principle first) and
what are your lawful bases and gateways that support
the sharing?
● How should you share the information (is it secure),
what is happening to the data at every stage?
● How will access to the information be limited to only
those who need to have access?
● How will you comply with transparency/informing
obligations?
● What quality checks are required to ensure the shared
data is accurate and up-to-date?
● What retention periods are appropriate? How is
destruction managed?

2 Purpose and benefits
How to complete
This section describes your sharing project/initiative
and clearly sets out the purpose(s) for which the
information is being shared.
This should be set out in clear and understandable
language, describing the:
● nature (what is planned to be done with the
personal data)
● scope (what does the processing cover – define the
agreed scope of sharing set out in the agreement and
outline how the data shared will be managed [i.e.
ensuring those that need it can see it, but those that
don’t, can’t])
● context (wider picture which might affect expectations
or impact)
● purposes for processing (reason why personal data will
be processed, benefit that the sharing will provide and/
or risks and possible consequences of not sharing and
how sharing information is necessary to achieve those
aims).
In these circumstances, ‘necessity’ with regard to justifying
why information is shared should be in the context of
‘reasonably necessary’, rather than strictly or absolutely
necessary. Sharing has to be a proportionate response to
the risk situation.
This section should set out what form the sharing
agreement is covering, such as formalised sharing via a
Multi-Agency Safeguarding Hub or in support of more ad
hoc sharing via the inter-agency communications that take
place around managing the welfare and safeguarding of
children, or both.
The section also links to a Data Protection Impact
Assessment (DPIA), often required in support of data
sharing and ideally performed early in the establishment of
the sharing initiative. Detail from the DPIA around purpose
may also be drawn into this section.
The purpose section of the sharing agreement is the
key section to be written by the relevant service leads
at each organisation. All partners/parties must agree on
the purpose.
This section can use text/language from other documents,
such as a business case, grant application or committee
report. This helps to ensure consistency.

Clearly set out your purpose(s) and benefits for sharing
(include a description of the nature, scope and context):
Research and experience has demonstrated the importance of information sharing across
professional boundaries to safeguard the welfare of children. The Children Act 2004
emphasises the importance of safeguarding children by stating that relevant partner agencies
– which include the police, local authority children services and NHS Trusts – must make
sure that functions are discharged with the aim of safeguarding and promoting the welfare of
children.
The Act also states that they must promote cooperation between relevant partner agencies
to improve the wellbeing of children in their area. Information necessary for safeguarding
decisions in relation to children and young people is held by numerous statutory and non-
statutory agencies. Many sad cases across the UK have highlighted deficiencies within
safeguarding partnerships in relation to the sharing of information and communication. Some
serious case reviews and inquiries (such as the Laming, Bichard and Baby P inquiries) have
directly attributed the lack of good information sharing and communication to the subsequent
death of an individual.
To deliver the best safeguarding decisions that ensure timely, necessary and proportionate
interventions, decision makers need the full information picture concerning an individual and
their circumstances to be available to them. Information viewed alone or in silos may not give
the full picture or identify the true risk. All the information from various agencies needs to be
available and accessible in one place; to keep children safe and assist signatories to this
Agreement in discharging their obligations under the Act and other legislation.
(Thanks to London Safeguarding Children Partnership for the above example)

2b Purpose and benefits – example scenarios
Scenario 1:
A local authority and mental health trust seek to
improve steps to prevent suicides by sharing personal
data concerning individuals considered to be at risk of
suicide. The police are also asked and agree to provide
relevant information but will not be accessing information
routinely (though may request or be informed of relevant
information on an ad-hoc basis).
The local authority and mental health trust have developed
an application available to care professionals they employ
in jointly provided suicide prevention services to manage
information they need. They have identified a number of
risk factors in their individual data sources that are known
to be related to suicide and by combining risk factors
from both organisations will have a fuller picture of risks
to individuals. The application will also help them identify
individuals who may be new to one service, but already
known to the other.
The information sharing is necessary so that the staff
involved with the individuals have the full picture of
current risks, concerns and services involved with them,
so can assess any change in circumstances and provide
advice, guidance and support as required to prevent
the risk of suicide and in time manage the care of the
individual to the point when they no longer need the
prevention service support. They have documented this in
a Data Sharing Agreement.
Scenario 2:
The Local Authorities Early Years Portage and Inclusion
Team provide support to families and early years
practitioners for children from birth to five years, who have
a disability and/or have special educational needs (SEN).
In order to provide the service needed, information sharing
between the local authority, the child’s setting and other
professionals who work with the child (e.g. GP, speech
therapist, SEN Team) takes place.
The information sharing is necessary to complete and
review educational progress over time and for planning
the effective delivery of the service. The information will
be used to fulfil the duties placed on local authorities;
namely for the provision and monitoring of support and
services for families with young children, and planning
future services and support, in the local area, and to
ensure that awareness, and availability, so families can
attend appropriate family support services. They have
documented this in a Data Sharing Agreement.

3 Data Controller(s) / Joint Controller(s)
How to complete
It is essential to identify and document the roles of
each organisation involved in any data sharing and
determine if they are a controller, joint controller
or processor (Processor(s) are covered in the next
section under 4 Processor(s)) for the personal data
being shared.
Data controllers decide why and how the personal data
will be processed and shared. We establish the status of
each organisation to help determine the actions and risks
for which each partner is responsible. Some scenarios
will be more straight forward than others. Please use the
help available on the following pages to establish the
relevant roles and use 3E to document any joint controller
arrangements (joint controllers must enter into an
arrangement under Article 26 of the UK GDPR).
It may also be useful to identify if organisations identified
as controllers contribute and/or view data (depending on
the sharing scenario). It is possible that some partners may
only view data provided by others. Such organisations
might be controllers if they are determining the purposes
and means of processing.
It is also possible that some organisations may be a
controller, but not contribute or access any personal
data. Whilst this is uncommon, if they are involved in
the determination of purposes and means, they are a
controller. An example may be an NHS Integrated Care
Board (ICB) that has been involved in the development
and governance of a Shared Care Record used by multiple
health and social care organisations that it commissions to
provide services.

3b Data controller(s) / joint controller(s) – example scenarios
Scenario 1: Joint controller arrangement:
Joint controller arrangement:
A local authority and mental health trust seek to
improve steps to prevent suicides by sharing personal
data concerning individuals considered to be at risk of
suicide. The local authority and mental health trust have
developed an application accessible over the internet to
care professionals they employ in jointly provided suicide
prevention services to manage information they need.
The Police are also asked and agree to share relevant
information but do not require access to health and
social care service information (though may request or
be informed of relevant information on an ad-hoc basis).
The local authority and mental health trust are joint
controllers of the data processed in the web application.
Scenario 2: Controller arrangement:
Controller arrangement:
A local authority, an acute NHS hospital trust and a
community mental health NHS trust establish a regular
multi-disciplinary team meeting to discuss a cohort of
individuals that they either are all, or may all be involved
with, providing complex care needs. The meeting is to
discuss both current caseload and potential individuals
to come on to the caseload. Each party brings notes to
the meeting and shares what they think is relevant at the
meeting. No joint notes are taken and no shared systems
are used. Each party might record in their own notes
some information from the others. In this circumstance
the organisations are likely to be separate controllers
exchanging data as required as they do not have a shared
asset (e.g. database) and whilst their objectives may be
linked they are not necessarily the same.

3c Data controller(s) / joint controller(s) – What your DPO will need to know
The DPO needs to know the relationships between all
the parties to a data sharing agreement, so they can help
establish each party’s responsibilities for complying with
the Data Protection Principles. Which organisations:
● have decided what data will be shared and how it will
be handled (determination of purposes and means)?
● will be contributing/sharing data?
● will be accessing/receiving data?
● will be handling/processing data on behalf of other
organisations (acting as data processors)?
A Data Protection Impact Assessment is very likely to be
required in many sharing scenarios and the DPO should be
actively involved in advising on this at an early stage in the
development.
Please also note that if a sharing initiative is a joint-
controller arrangement the Data Sharing Agreement (and
all other relevant information governance documentation)
must be completed jointly.

5 Data shared between partners
How to complete
Service leads, operational staff and analytical staff
who understand the information should work
with the information governance team and the
organisation’s DPO to decide what data/information
is necessary and proportionate to share, to achieve
the stated purpose.
The main considerations here are justification (why do
we need this information and does the law allow us to
process it for the intended purpose(s) and proportionality
(how much information do we need and is there a way to
reach the goal with less or pseudonymised or anonymised
data). For details about the different levels of identifiability
please see Toolkit 2.
If your sharing initiative is straight-forward you will be
able to list the required details directly in the data sharing
agreement. If the intended sharing of information is in
a more informal way (corridor conversations) please see
Toolkit 1 for further details.
Some (maybe less formal) sharing initiatives might
require you to have a conversation to establish what
information should be considered to be shared. Partner
organisations requesting data for safeguarding reasons do
not necessarily understand what information is available to
be shared.
Therefore it is important to push (what do you have that
other parties are not asking for but in your view they need
to know) and pull (what are the parties asking for) in order
to get to the required results faster and safer. It is good
practice to include this in the Data Sharing Agreement to
document good practice.
What is personal data?
Personal data relates to a directly or indirectly identified or
identifiable living individual. What identifies an individual
could be as simple as a name or a number or could
include other identifiers such as an IP address or a cookie
identifier, or other factors. If identifiers are being removed
from data in order to pseudonymise the data it is still
personal data under the UK GDPR. Please see Toolkit 2 for
more details around levels of identifiability.

6 Lawfulness – UK GDPR lawful basis and Article 9 condition
How to complete
All personal data processing must comply with both
the UK GDPR, and the Data Protection Act 2018
(DPA 2018). If the purpose can be achieved without
the processing of personal data (e.g. by processing
anonymous data) you will not have a lawful basis
to process.
To process personal data you must identify a lawful basis
listed in the UK GDPR (Article 6). The type of data, the
purposes for processing, and the relationship with the
individual (data subject) will impact which lawful basis
is appropriate.
If you are processing special category personal data (listed
in section 5 guidance) you must also meet a lawful basis
condition in Article 9.
When processing special category data, you must also
identify a condition from Schedule 1 Part 1, or Schedule 1
Part 2 of the DPA 2018 (see section 6a and guidance).
For processing personal data related to criminal
convictions and offences (known as Law Enforcement
processing) Article 10 of the UK GDPR applies, and a
condition from the DPA 2018 Schedule 1 Part 3 must be
identified. For more detail please see Law Enforcement
section on page 41.
Please use the lawful basis interactive guidance tool (refer
to 6d) provided by the ICO and the help available on the
following pages to assist you.
Identifying the appropriate lawful basis helps to meet
Principle 1 of the GDPR, for fair and lawful processing
of personal data. The lawful basis affects how the rights
of the data subject apply, and will determine how you
meet your obligations to be transparent with a data
subject about how their data is processed and shared. For
example, choosing 6(1)(c) for processing under a legal
obligation, removes the right to erasure, to portability and
to object.
An Appropriate Policy Document (APD) is required when
using most of the public interest conditions under the
DPA 2018. If you are processing under Article 9(2)(g) for
Safeguarding reasons it is very likely that you will require
an APD (please see 6d if you require further details).
If you establish legitimate interests as your most
appropriate basis in law you must complete a legitimate
interests assessment to help you demonstrate compliance
if required. Please find details on this under 6d,
Legitimate Interests.

7 Non-interference – common law duty of confidentiality
How to complete
There are three general approaches to sharing personal
data without interference with the common law duty of
confidentiality. These are if you have consent from the
individual (data subject), if there is a substantial public
interest which allows you to process the information in
question or if there is a legal duty (e.g., a court order
in place asking you to disclose information or clear
duty to share in legislation). You should utilise the most
appropriate condition for the circumstances.
It is of note that consent under the Common Law Duty
of Confidentiality is not the same then consent as a
lawful basis under data protection law. Consent can
be implied and is a valid basis under the common law
duty of confidentiality (in contrary to consent under
data protection law which must be freely given, specific,
and involve an affirmative action (UK GDPR Article 7
compliance)). This can be achieved by the individual(s)
being made aware (informed) at which point they can
raise queries or concerns if they have any.
The conditions to be met for the common law duty
of confidentiality are different to the conditions for
processing under UK GDPR. However, you can consider
them in a similar way, and use the GDPR conditions to
describe why the common law duty is met.
Local authorities and their sharing partners will very
likely share information with each other on the basis of
public interest for safeguarding children. This includes
the protection of individuals from neglect or physical,
mental, or emotional harm but also the protection of
physical, mental or emotional well-being of an individual.
This doesn’t mean individuals should not be informed
and engaged about the sharing of their data, but that
they should understand why it needs to be done. The
best approach in general in safeguarding (or potential)
circumstances is to consider sharing on the basis of
substantial public interests and when doing so inform the
relevant individual(s) about what, why and who, unless the
circumstances are such that to inform could increase the
risk to individual(s).

7A Non-interference – Human Rights Act 1998, Article 8
How to complete
The Human Rights Act 1998, Article 8 (HRA) sets out the
fundamental rights and freedoms that everyone in the
UK is entitled to (e.g. the right to marry or the right to be
protected from discrimination). It gives public authorities
a legal framework to help them to assure that everything
they do respects the human rights of the individuals they
are providing services for. Article 8 is the right to respect
for private and family life (privacy) and is relevant when
considering information sharing (are we assured of non-
interference with everyone’s right to respect for private
and family life, their home and their correspondence?).
The right of respect for private and family life may be
restricted. If so, please state why this is necessary and if
it is proportionate (e.g. necessary if balanced against the
protection of the rights and freedoms of others, not more
than required to achieve the purpose for sharing).
In practice the right to respect for private and family life is
very similar to the common law duty of confidentiality and
the same general bases that support data sharing under
the common law will support interference with the right
to privacy.
Article 8 states: There shall be no interference by a public
authority with the exercise of this right except such as is in
accordance with the law and is necessary in a democratic
society in the interests of national security, public safety or
the economic wellbeing of the country, for the prevention
of disorder or crime, for the protection of health or
morals, or for the protection of the rights and freedoms of
others.
There are clear links in safeguarding circumstances with
the protection of health, the protection of rights (e.g.
right to life, prohibition of torture) and in some cases the
prevention of crime.

9 Individual rights
How to complete
Every individual is entitled to have their personal
information protected, used in a fair and lawful way, and
made available to them when they ask for a copy. If an
individual feels that their personal information is factually
incorrect they are entitled to ask for that information to be
corrected.
The responsibility to comply with individual rights requests
and therefore to establish how they are managed lawfully
lays with the controllers of the information being shared/
processed and must be taken seriously.
Processes must be agreed with all controllers (if more
than one) to allow individuals to exercise their rights
in a straight forward and safe way. Emphasis must be
put on managing requests considering exemptions (e.g.
disclosure of third party information or information which
could cause severe harm and distress); following legal
requirements (e.g. legal timeframes, providing regulator
contact details); and allowing case by case assessments
involving the relevant parties, but at the same time making
it easy and understandable for individuals to exercise
their rights.
Sharing partners may be able to specifically state an
agreed process for how to manage individual rights
(including subject access request) if agreed in advance.
Processes documentation (e.g. process map, standard
operating procedures (SOPs)) can be added to the Data
Sharing Agreement as an appendix.
In terms of safeguarding children, any processes must be
set out with the safeguarding of the child at the centre.
Requests to exercise data protection rights may come from
children, or often someone with parental responsibility.
Consideration must be given to the age and capacity of
the child when the request comes from someone with
parental responsibility. The Data Sharing Agreement must
link to sufficient partner organisational policy and process
to deal with such requests and detail how these are
tackled in a collaborative manner where data is shared.
NB where the example refers to ‘system’ that can be taken
to mean a shared database either set up specifically or
where partners provide access to each other’s systems.

Scenario 1:

A group of public sector organisations from across health,

monthly ‘data sharing’ meeting and have a set of criteria

that may benefit from a multi-disciplinary discussion
to ensure these individuals are offered and receiving
appropriate support.

that they run on their organisational systems to highlight
the particular individuals and provide a summary of
data on each. This is sometimes complemented by staff
reviewing the individuals’ records and noting other key

Toolkit 1: Informal sharing
‘Informal’ sharing refers to sharing that needs to
take place between professionals that isn’t via a
shared system/database or via a formal committee
(e.g. MDT meeting, MASH etc). Informal sharing may
well lead onto more formalised actions or may come
from procedural sharing via a system or database.
In essence this relates to the ‘corridor conversation’
between a nurse and a social worker, or police
officer etc.
When faced with an opportunity to ask or tell…
You may well find yourself with the opportunity to ask or
tell a colleague (internal or external) for information on a
child(ren) and family you are involved with. In this situation
consider:
● Would the child(ren)/family reasonably expect you to
speak to your colleague about them?
● If they may not reasonably expect you to do so, but you
feel the colleague is likely to be able to help you ensure
their wellbeing or their safety, or you need to tell them
something to assist with their involvement, then you
should have the conversation with your colleague.
When seeking an opportunity to ask or tell…
This is similar to the above, but when the conversation is
more planned, i.e, deciding to call or email a contact. The
only difference is that if you are unsure about whether
the child(ren)/family would reasonably expect you to have
the conversation and you can’t easily justify having the
conversation without their knowledge, then there is the
opportunity to have a discussion with them first, if that is
appropriate. This isn’t to get their consent but to ensure
they are informed. If the circumstances are urgent and
delay may increase the risk in a safeguarding context or
in fact discussion with the family may also increase the
risk, then that is a robust basis to have the conversation
without involvement of the child(ren)/family.
When asked about something you know….
As with the above circumstances this may be an
unplanned conversation or one you’ve scheduled to
have. The same approach will generally apply, when
you are asked, consider if the child(ren)/family would
reasonably expect you to answer or if the circumstances
of the case warrant and urgent response or if seeking
family involvement would potentially increase risk to the
child(ren).
Having a conversation, not a question and answer
session…
Sharing information is critical to effective safeguarding.
Many agencies involved can be said to have a ‘need to
know’. However, if the sharing is simply a question and
answer session, then key information may not get shared.

If you decide that the requestor needs to know the answer
you can give them, also think around the wider context of
what you know, that they may need to be told, which may

Checklist of things to consider:
● Do you need to identify who you are talking about? Can

have.
● Do discuss whether data can be shared beyond the
conversation you are having, so others are aware of
your views on that aspect.
● Document the sharing as soon as possible, by making
appropriate notes about what you’ve shared and why
and also what you have been told (this may be a note

Toolkit 2: Levels of identifiability
Article 5 (c) of UK GDPR requires that personal data
shall be ‘adequate, relevant and limited to what is
necessary in relation to the purposes for which they
are processed (‘data minimisation’)’.
You must only use the minimum amount of personal data
necessary to achieve the stated purpose, and controllers
must describe why they need the specified data. The
purpose may be achieved using pseudonymised or
anonymised data.
Reducing the level of identifying factors in any data to
the minimum required reduces the risk to privacy and the
likelihood of a damaging impact if mistakes are made
with the data. For example, if a dataset contains a social
care reference number for each person and details of their
social care needs, but no detail on their name, specific
address or other identifiers, then if the dataset is sent to
the wrong person, the likelihood of damage or distress
being caused is reduced.
Controllers must consider whether individuals may still be
identifiable by others, even where it is not required nor
justified in respect of the purpose for processing. Despite
steps to pseudonymise/anonymise data, the context of the
data, i.e. a combination of information and/or low numbers
of individuals, means that identification of individuals is
possible. You should take further steps to mitigate the risk
of identification, such as restricting access to processing
outputs or suppressing data with low numbers.
Personal data
Data that can be used to identify an individual, such as:
● Name
● Date of birth
● NHS number
● Home address
● National Insurance number
● Employee reference number
Pseudonymised data
Pseudonymisation refers to techniques that replace,
remove or transform information that identifies
individuals, and keep that information separate. The
data is changed so that the personal data can no longer
be attributed to a specific data subject without the use
of additional information, provided that such additional
information is kept separately and is subject to technical
and organisational measures to ensure that the personal
data are not attributed to an identified or identifiable
natural person. Techniques include replacing personal data
with a unique reference that is restricted to only those that
need to be able to identify the individual. Pseudonymised
data is still considered personal data under the UK GDPR.

Anonymised data
UK GDPR (recital 26) defines anonymous data as:
information which does not relate to an identified or
identifiable natural person or to personal data rendered
anonymous in such a manner that the data subject is not
or no longer identifiable.
Effectively anonymised data
There is a correlation between ‘effectively anonymised
data’ and ‘pseudonymised data’. However ‘effectively
anonymised’ is a wider form of data. In pseudonymised
data, all or most of the identifiers have been removed and
replaced with a pseudonym (often a reference number)
that as few organisations and individuals as possible can
reverse if needs be. ‘Effectively anonymised data’ is data
that has had identifiers removed to the point that it is
unlikely to be identifiable as risk of re-identification is
sufficiently remote, taking account of the context, scope
and purposes of what the data is used for, technical
measures to protect the data, the environment in which
the data is to be disclosed.
Identifiable in context
Where it is not intended for individuals to be identified
but it is possible to identify individuals from the data/
information included. For example, it may be possible to
identify an individual from a description or by something
specific that they are quoted to have said that others
would have heard them say. It may be possible to identify
someone through a combination of data, such as a
postcode combined with equalities data like ethnicity,
health condition or religion.
Low number suppression
Individuals may be identified when data/information
is attributed/linked and refers to a small number of
individuals. For example, the following data may clearly be
linked to an individual if they are the only person of the
characteristics living in a particular place: male, Chinese,
prosthetic arm, living in postcode district XX11.
In such instances results are often suppressed and reported
as “less than 5” or categories can be merged, age brackets
increased, etc.

Toolkit 3: What is a Data Protection Impact
Assessment (DPIA)
(From the ICO’s guide to UK GDPR)
You must do a DPIA for processing that is likely to result in
a high risk to the rights and freedoms of individuals. This
includes some specified types of processing. You can use
screening checklists to help you decide when to do a DPIA.
It is also good practice to do a DPIA for any other major
project which requires the processing of personal data.
Your DPIA must:
● describe the nature, scope, context and purposes of the
processing;
● assess necessity, proportionality and compliance
measures;
● identify and assess risks to individuals; and
● identify any additional measures to mitigate those risks.
To assess the level of risk, you must consider both the
likelihood and the severity of any impact on individuals.
High risk could result from either a high probability of
some harm, or a lower possibility of serious harm. Risk
might be unnecessary intrusion into an individual’s privacy,
or the potential for physical or mental harm if the data
were used inappropriately.
You must consult your data protection officer (if you have
one) and, where appropriate, individuals and relevant
experts. Any processors may also need to assist you.
If you identify a high risk that you cannot mitigate, you
must consult the ICO before starting the processing.

Toolkit 4: Digital Economy Act 2017
From the ICO’s ‘Data sharing across the public sector:
the Digital Economy Act codes’:
Part 5 of the Digital Economy Act 2017 (DEA) focuses
on digital government, providing gateways that allow
specified public authorities to share data with each other.
Some of these gateways enable the sharing of personal
data, while others allow the sharing of non-identifying
data. The objectives and purposes for data sharing
under the DEA powers are tightly defined.
Under the DEA you must still comply with the data
protection legislation.
Part 5 of the DEA explicitly:
● States that all processing of information under the DEA
powers must comply with data protection legislation;
and
● prohibits the disclosure of information where it would
contravene data protection legislation.
“Examples of where the DEA may be used as a legal
gateway for sharing data include:
● where the purpose is the improvement of the well-being
of individuals or households (including physical, mental
and emotional well-being of children).
Case example
Somerset Council have registered an Data Sharing
Agreement with the DEA as its legal gateway for the
following purpose:
Providing early intervention opportunities and presenting
multi-agency data pertaining to social issues to a range
of frontline professionals. The integration and sharing
of this joined up data will elicit a deeper and broader
understanding of the multiple issues affecting individuals/
families.
It will reduce duplication and resource for practitioners
supporting the family, as it contains headline detail on
assessments and current and historic involvement, not just
with statutory services, but commissioned providers. It will:
● identify families facing multiple disadvantages
● enable the improvement or targeting of appropriate
support to individuals or households
● enable the improvement of the physical, mental,
emotional, social or economic wellbeing of individuals
and families”

Toolkit 5: Consent considerations
Consent under the UK General Data Protection
Regulation is just one lawful basis for processing of
personal data.
It is unlikely for you to require consent in data protection
law to share data about a child, young person or family
member if the sharing is for safeguarding reasons.
However, you do need a lawful basis in data protection
legislation to share personal data, but consent is just
one of the options available. Instead, it is likely that,
depending upon the type of organisation you are and
the circumstances, the appropriate lawful bases will be
public task, legitimate interests, legal obligation or vital
interests. The ICO’s interactive lawful basis guidance tool
and guidance on Consent | ICO will help you to choose
the right one.
When is consent inappropriate?
It follows that if for any reason you cannot offer people a
genuine choice over how you use their data, consent will
not be the appropriate basis for processing. This may be
the case if, for example:
● you would still process the data on a different lawful
basis if consent were refused or withdrawn;
● you ask for ‘consent’ to the processing as a precondition
of accessing your services; or
● you are in a position of power over the individual – for
example, if you are a public authority or an employer
processing employee data.
A common issue related to consent is the
misunderstanding about consent under data protection
law and consent in a different context, such as the
provision of services by a local authority or the provision
of healthcare by the NHS. Consent to receive treatment
or a service offered by an organisation or team is different
from consent as a lawful basis under data protection law.
As stated earlier, it is unlikely to be appropriate to use
consent as a lawful basis under data protection law; other
lawful bases will be more suitable.
It is important to keep in mind that whichever lawful basis
the processing of personal data is based on does not alter
the requirement to inform individuals of how their data
is being used (unless there is a legitimate reason not to
inform them).
